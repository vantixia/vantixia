Deepfakes and Synthetic Media: The New Face of Fraud

Seeing is no longer believing. A cloned voice or a fabricated video call is now a viable path to a fraudulent wire transfer.

Cyber Defense By RAJNISH KUMAR • 08 Jan 2026 • 7 min read

For most of human history, seeing and hearing were reasonable proxies for believing. A familiar voice on the phone, a face on a video call, a recognisable executive delivering an instruction - these carried an intuitive weight of authenticity. Synthetic media has quietly dissolved that assumption. A convincing clone of a voice can now be produced from a short sample, and a fabricated video of a real person is no longer the exclusive domain of film studios. Fraud has taken notice.

The danger of deepfakes is not primarily that they fool us in the abstract. It is that they slot neatly into scams that already work, replacing the weakest forged element - proof that the request is really coming from a trusted person - with something far more persuasive.

How the illusion is built

At a high level, synthetic media is generated by models trained on examples of a target's voice or face until they can produce new, plausible output in that likeness. The barrier to entry has fallen steadily: less source material is needed, the results are more convincing, and real-time generation is increasingly feasible. The practical upshot for a defender is simple - assume that a voice or a face, on its own, can be manufactured, and that the quality will keep improving.

Where the fraud actually lands

The most damaging uses follow the money and the trust. The pattern classes worth understanding are:

  • Executive impersonation. A cloned voice or video of a senior leader instructs a staff member to make an urgent payment or share sensitive information, exploiting authority and time pressure.
  • Identity-verification bypass. Systems that rely on a face or voice as proof of identity become targets when that biometric can be synthesised.
  • Reputation and disinformation. Fabricated media of a real person saying or doing something they never did, used to manipulate, extort, or deceive.

Why detection alone will not save you

It is tempting to hope for a technical detector that reliably flags fakes. Detection tools exist and help, but they are locked in an arms race with generation, and a defence that depends on staying ahead of a fast-moving generator is a fragile one. Treating "can we detect it" as the whole answer sets you up to lose the moment the fakes improve - which they continually do.

Defend the process, not just the pixels

The durable defences are procedural, because they do not depend on spotting the fake at all. Require that consequential actions - large payments, changes to banking details, releases of sensitive data - be confirmed through a second, independent channel rather than acting on a single call or message. Agree on verification steps in advance, such as call-backs to known numbers or shared code words for high-value requests. And build awareness: staff who know that a familiar voice can be faked are far harder to rush into a mistake than staff who assume voices cannot lie.

The bottom line

Deepfakes do not invent new crimes; they supercharge old ones by faking the trust that scams have always needed. Because detection will always trail generation, the real defence is process - independent confirmation for anything that moves money or data, pre-agreed verification, and a workforce that no longer treats a voice or a face as proof. Stop trying to win the "is it real" arms race, and start making authenticity something you verify rather than something you assume.
