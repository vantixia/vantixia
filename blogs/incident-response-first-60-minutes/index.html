Incident Response: The First 60 Minutes Matter Most

How you spend the first hour of an incident decides how the next month goes. Panic is expensive; a plan is priceless.

Cyber Defence By RAJNISH KUMAR • 20 Jan 2026 • 7 min read

Every incident has an hour zero: the moment an alert stops being background noise and someone says, out loud, that something is genuinely wrong. What happens in the following sixty minutes shapes the outcome more than almost anything that comes later. Move well, and you limit the intruder's reach while preserving the evidence you'll need. Move badly, and you can destroy your own ability to understand what happened – sometimes doing the attacker's work of spreading chaos for them.

First, establish what you're actually looking at

The instinct to act immediately is strong and usually wrong. The first job is triage: is this a single compromised laptop, a malicious insider, or a foothold on a server that touches everything? Which accounts, systems, and data are plausibly involved? A few minutes spent scoping changes every decision that follows, because containing the wrong thing squanders your only real advantage – that the attacker may not yet know they've been noticed.

Write things down from the first minute. A running timeline of what was observed, when, and what was done about it feels bureaucratic in the moment and becomes priceless within days – for investigators, insurers, regulators, and your own post-incident review.

Contain without burning the evidence

Containment and evidence preservation pull in opposite directions, and the first hour is where they collide. Pulling the power on a compromised machine destroys everything in memory – running processes, network connections, encryption keys – which may be exactly what the investigation needs. Isolating the machine from the network while leaving it powered on usually achieves containment while keeping that volatile evidence intact.

The same tension applies to accounts. Disabling the one obviously compromised account may simply tell the intruder to switch to the three others you haven't found yet. Deliberate, coordinated containment – done once, done broadly – beats a piecemeal response that teaches the attacker how you defend.

Say the right things to the right people

The first hour needs an incident commander: one named person who makes decisions and delegates, so that ten well-meaning responders don't take ten conflicting actions. It also needs a communication channel that isn't the environment under attack – if corporate email or chat may be compromised, coordinating the response there hands the intruder a transcript of your defence.

Keep early communication factual and narrow. Speculation travels fast inside a company, and premature external statements are almost impossible to retract. Legal, communications, and leadership need to be informed early; they do not need to run the technical response.

The mistakes that echo for weeks

Most first-hour damage is self-inflicted, and it follows familiar patterns:

  • Reimaging affected machines immediately, erasing the evidence that would have shown how the attacker got in.
  • Forcing mass password resets before understanding the foothold, disrupting the business while missing the persistence the intruder actually relies on.
  • Coordinating the response over systems the attacker can read.
  • Tipping off the intruder with noisy, partial containment, prompting them to accelerate or destroy.
  • Keeping no timeline, leaving the organisation unable to reconstruct its own response afterwards.

The takeaway

None of this can be improvised well at speed. The organisations that handle hour one calmly are the ones that rehearsed it: a written playbook, named roles and deputies, out-of-band channels agreed in advance, and at least one tabletop exercise that tested the plan against a plausible scenario. The first sixty minutes will be stressful either way – the only question is whether the chaos runs the response, or the plan does.
