Password Hygiene in the Passkey Era

The password isn't dead yet, but passkeys are writing the obituary. Here is how to bridge the two worlds safely.

Cyber Defense By RAJNISH KUMAR • 09 Feb 2026 • 6 min read

The password has been declared dead so many times that its obituary is a genre of its own. Yet here we are: nearly every system still accepts one, most people still juggle dozens, and stolen credentials remain among the most common ways attackers walk through the front door. The honest picture is a transition, not a funeral - passkeys are arriving, passwords are lingering, and good hygiene has to cover both.

Reuse is the real killer

The most damaging password habit is not weakness but repetition. When one website is breached and its credential database leaks, attackers feed those email-and-password pairs into automated tools that try them against banking, email, corporate and shopping sites at scale - an attack class called credential stuffing. A password that is long, complex and reused is still a skeleton key the moment any one site holding it gets compromised. Uniqueness per account matters more than any cleverness within a single password.

Managers and passphrases: the workable present

Nobody can memorize a unique, random password for every account, and nobody should try. A password manager solves the problem structurally: it generates long random strings, stores them encrypted, and fills them only on the matching site - which also quietly protects against lookalike phishing domains, since the manager will not offer credentials to the wrong address.

The one secret you must still remember is the master credential, and there the guidance has genuinely changed. Modern standards, including guidance from NIST, favor long memorable passphrases over short strings of forced symbols, and drop mandatory periodic rotation - frequent forced changes push people toward predictable patterns, while a strong passphrase changed only upon suspicion of compromise holds up better. Length is the property that resists guessing; four or five unrelated words beat a contorted eight-character puzzle.

What a passkey actually is

Passkeys are built on the WebAuthn standard, and the mechanics explain their appeal. When you register, your device creates a mathematically linked pair of keys: a public key stored by the website and a private key that never leaves your device or its synced keychain. Signing in means proving possession of the private key, unlocked locally by your fingerprint, face or device PIN - the biometric never travels anywhere.

The security consequences are profound. There is no shared secret for a breached website to leak, nothing for you to type into a counterfeit login page, and the credential is bound to the genuine site's identity, so a convincing fake gets nothing. Phishing, stuffing and database theft - the three great password plagues - simply lose their targets.

Migrating without the mess

No organization flips to passkeys overnight, and pretending otherwise causes lockouts and shadow workarounds. A realistic path looks like this:

  • Enable passkeys alongside passwords on your identity provider and highest-value applications first, letting users enroll gradually.
  • Harden account recovery before you rely on passkeys - a strong credential guarded by a weak reset flow is theater.
  • Consolidate applications behind single sign-on so each passkey rollout covers many systems at once.
  • Screen remaining passwords against known-breach lists and retire forced rotation policies that no longer earn their friction.
  • Keep a documented, tightly controlled fallback for lost devices, and test it.

Where passwords will stubbornly remain

Even an aggressive migration leaves a long tail: legacy internal applications that predate WebAuthn, network hardware and infrastructure consoles, service and machine accounts, shared credentials for team resources, and break-glass administrator logins reserved for emergencies. These stragglers deserve the strongest treatment precisely because they cannot benefit from the new model - vault them in a manager, make them long and unique, restrict who can retrieve them, and log every use.

The short version

Password hygiene in the passkey era means running two disciplines at once. For the accounts that support passkeys, enroll and enjoy credentials that cannot be phished or leaked. For everything else, let a manager enforce uniqueness, let length do the heavy lifting, and guard the recovery paths that attackers now consider the softest target. The era is transitional; your standards should not be.
