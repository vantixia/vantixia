Natural Language to Detection Rules: Writing Sigma With AI

Describe the behaviour in plain English; get a draft detection back. AI is shrinking the gap between an idea and a working rule.

AI for Security By RAJNISH KUMAR • 01 Mar 2026 • 6 min read

Detection engineering has a bottleneck that has nothing to do with ideas and everything to do with translation. An analyst reads a threat report, understands the behaviour perfectly, and then spends an hour wrestling that understanding into the exact syntax of a Sigma rule, a YARA signature, or a KQL query. The knowledge was there in minutes; the working detection took the rest of the afternoon. Language models are shrinking that gap by turning a plain-English description of malicious behaviour into a first draft of a rule.

Used well, this is a genuine accelerant for the blue team. Used carelessly, it is a fast way to fill your SIEM with confident, broken, noisy detections. The difference is entirely in what you do after the model hands you a draft.

Describe the behaviour, get a draft

The workflow is straightforward and powerful. You describe what you want to catch - "a Windows process spawning a scripting engine that then makes an outbound connection to a rare domain" - and the model produces a candidate rule in the target format, with the fields, conditions, and logic roughly in place. For engineers who do not live inside Sigma or KQL syntax every day, this collapses the distance between recognising a technique and expressing it as a detection. It democratises detection engineering beyond the handful of people fluent in each query language.

The non-negotiable step: test before you trust

Here is where discipline separates value from damage. An AI-drafted rule is a hypothesis, not a detection. It may reference a field that does not exist in your data, encode logic that never fires, or match so broadly that it buries analysts in false positives. Every generated rule must be validated against real data before it goes anywhere near production - confirmed to fire on true positives, tuned against a representative sample to measure its false-positive rate, and reviewed by someone who understands both the technique and your environment.

Treat detections like code

The same practices that keep software sane keep AI-assisted detections sane.

  • Version control. Every rule lives in a repository with history, review, and rollback - not pasted straight into a console.
  • Human review. A second set of eyes on the logic catches the plausible-but-wrong conditions models love to produce.
  • Tuning as a first-class task. A rule's job is not to fire; it is to fire on the right things, which means iterating on thresholds and exclusions after you see real-world behaviour.

Where the human still matters most

A model can draft a rule, but it does not know your environment - which processes are normal for your business, which domains your applications legitimately talk to, where the noise lives. That context is exactly what turns a generic rule into a useful one, and it is exactly what the model lacks. The engineer's job shifts from writing syntax to supplying context and judgement, which is a better use of their expertise anyway.

The bottom line

Natural-language-to-detection is a real productivity win: it lets analysts move from an idea to a draft rule in seconds and opens detection engineering to more of the team. But a draft is not a detection until it has been tested against real data, tuned for your environment, reviewed by a human, and committed to version control. Keep that discipline and AI makes your detection engineering faster. Skip it and it just makes your false positives faster.
