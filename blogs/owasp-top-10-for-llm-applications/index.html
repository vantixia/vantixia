The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications, Explained

OWASP mapped the ten risks that matter most for large language model apps. Here is a practitioner's tour of each, with what to actually do about it.

AI Security By RAJNISH KUMAR • 23 Jul 2026 • 9 min read

When OWASP published a Top 10 specifically for LLM applications, the reaction from seasoned appsec people was mild déjà vu - half the entries are old friends wearing new clothes. That is the point. LLM systems recombine familiar failure modes (injection, over-privileged components, unverified dependencies) with a few genuinely new ones, and the list gives builders and testers a shared map. We use it to scope engagements; you should use it to scope reviews.

The ten risks, each with the control that matters most

Here is the list as we apply it in practice - one risk, one primary control:

  • LLM01 - Prompt injection. Untrusted text steers the model, directly or via content it processes. Assume it succeeds, and cap the blast radius with least privilege and validation enforced outside the model.
  • LLM02 - Insecure output handling. Model output flows unexamined into browsers, shells, or SQL, reviving XSS and injection one hop downstream. Treat generated text exactly like user input: encode, parameterize, sandbox.
  • LLM03 - Training data poisoning. Corrupted training or fine-tuning data implants biases or backdoors. Demand provenance for datasets and verify what you ingest before it shapes weights.
  • LLM04 - Model denial of service. Expensive prompts - huge contexts, recursive expansions - burn compute and budget. Enforce token ceilings, timeouts, and per-client quotas.
  • LLM05 - Supply chain vulnerabilities. Pretrained models, adapters, and datasets from public hubs arrive unverified. Pin versions, check hashes and signatures, and evaluate third-party models before they touch production.
  • LLM06 - Sensitive information disclosure. Models reveal secrets absorbed from training data or loaded into their context. Keep secrets out of prompts and training sets, and filter outputs for known-sensitive patterns.
  • LLM07 - Insecure plugin design. Tools and plugins accept free-form model output as commands with no validation or authorization of their own. Every plugin must authenticate its caller and treat parameters as hostile.
  • LLM08 - Excessive agency. The system can do more than its purpose requires - too many tools, too much scope, too little oversight. Grant the minimum capability set and gate irreversible actions on human approval.
  • LLM09 - Overreliance. Humans and downstream systems accept fluent output as correct, shipping hallucinated code and citations. Build verification into the workflow; fluency is not accuracy.
  • LLM10 - Model theft. Weights exfiltrated through storage compromise or approximated through mass querying. Protect model artifacts like crown-jewel IP and watch for extraction-scale query patterns.

Read it as three families, not ten line items

The list compresses nicely. LLM01, LLM03, and LLM05 are trust failures on the way in - prompts, data, and artifacts accepted without verification. LLM02, LLM07, and LLM08 are trust failures on the way out - output and capability wired to consequences without checks. LLM04, LLM06, LLM09, and LLM10 are what the asset itself invites: resource abuse, leakage, misplaced confidence, theft. Seen that way, two engineering habits cover most of the surface: verify everything that enters the model's world, and constrain everything that leaves it.

Turning the list into a review checklist

The practical move is a one-hour exercise. Put your architecture diagram on a screen, walk the ten entries, and mark where each risk physically lives - which component, which team, which repo. Then assign an owner per entry. In our experience the orphans are predictable: LLM02 falls between the ML team ("we produce text") and the app team ("we just render it"), and LLM08 belongs to nobody because tool permissions were set once during a hackathon. Re-run the exercise whenever a new tool, plugin, or data source joins the pipeline - with LLM systems, that is a monthly event, not an annual one.

The bottom line

The OWASP LLM Top 10 will not make your system secure - no checklist does - but it makes an unfamiliar attack surface legible. Ten entries, three families, two habits: verify what goes in, constrain what comes out. Walk your architecture against it once and you will find at least one risk nobody owns; fixing that ownership gap is worth more than any single technical control on the list.
