The Anatomy of a Ransomware Attack, Step by Step

Ransomware rarely detonates on day one. It is the loud finale of a quiet intrusion you could have caught days earlier.

Cyber Defense By RAJNISH KUMAR • 13 Feb 2026 • 8 min read

Ransomware is often imagined as a single explosive moment: files locked, a ransom note glowing on every screen. In reality, that moment is the finale of a campaign that typically unfolds over days or weeks, most of it silent. Understanding the stages in order matters, because each one is an opportunity to stop the attack before it becomes a headline.

Getting in and settling down

Every ransomware operation begins with initial access. The common routes are unglamorous: a phishing email carrying a malicious attachment or link, a stolen or purchased set of remote-access credentials, an exposed remote desktop service, or an unpatched internet-facing application. Increasingly, specialized criminal groups known as initial access brokers handle this stage alone, then sell working footholds to ransomware operators.

Once inside, the intruder establishes persistence - a way to survive reboots and password changes. That might mean installing a remote-access tool, creating new accounts, or scheduling tasks that quietly relaunch their malware. At this point nothing is encrypted, nothing is obviously broken, and most victims have no idea anything is wrong.

Climbing the ladder, spreading sideways

A foothold on one workstation is worth little. The attacker's next objective is privilege escalation: harvesting cached credentials, exploiting local vulnerabilities, or abusing misconfigured permissions until they hold administrator rights. Armed with those, they move laterally - hopping from machine to machine using legitimate administrative tools, which makes the activity blend into normal IT traffic.

The prize is control of central infrastructure, especially identity systems and backup servers. Mature ransomware crews deliberately locate and disable or delete backups before doing anything visible, precisely because backups are the victim's best escape route. This phase is where defenders have the richest detection opportunities, and where too few are looking.

Theft before the lock: double extortion

Modern operations rarely rely on encryption alone. Before triggering it, attackers exfiltrate sensitive data - contracts, financial records, customer information, source code - to infrastructure they control. This enables double extortion: pay to decrypt your systems, and pay again to prevent publication of the stolen data. It is a deliberate answer to good backups; even a victim who can restore everything still faces the threat of a public leak. Large, unusual outbound data transfers are therefore one of the last reliable warnings before the finale.

The encryption event and the squeeze

When the attacker judges their position strong enough, encryption is deployed broadly and fast, often outside business hours to delay response. Ransom notes appear, sometimes accompanied by pressure tactics: countdown timers, escalating demands, calls to executives, or notices to the victim's customers and partners. The negotiation that follows is run like a business transaction, because for the operators, it is one - many groups function as ransomware-as-a-service platforms, with developers, affiliates and negotiators splitting proceeds.

Where defenders break the chain

Because the campaign has so many stages, defenders get multiple chances to interrupt it. The highest-value breakpoints include:

  • Hardening the entry points: patching internet-facing systems, closing exposed remote-access services, and filtering malicious email.
  • Detecting the quiet middle: alerting on unusual credential use, new persistence mechanisms and lateral movement between systems that rarely talk.
  • Protecting backups specifically: keeping copies offline or immutable, separating backup credentials from domain administration, and testing restores regularly.
  • Watching data flows: flagging large or abnormal outbound transfers before exfiltration completes.
  • Rehearsing the response: knowing in advance who isolates what, who decides, and who communicates, so containment starts in minutes rather than days.

The takeaway

Ransomware succeeds not because encryption is unstoppable but because the long, noisy approach that precedes it goes unnoticed. Every stage - access, persistence, escalation, lateral movement, exfiltration - leaves traces, and any one of them, caught in time, collapses the entire operation. Organizations that treat the encryption event as the beginning of the story respond too late by definition. Treat it as the end of a chain you can see and cut, and the odds shift meaningfully in your favor.
