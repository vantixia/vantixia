Threat Modeling Machine Learning Systems

You threat-model your web app. Your model deserves the same. ML systems have attack surfaces classic threat models never enumerate.

AI Security By RAJNISH KUMAR • 21 Jun 2026 • 8 min read

You would not ship a web application without asking who might attack it, what they would want, and where the weak joints are. Machine learning systems get deployed with that question skipped constantly, usually because the people building them are data scientists optimising for accuracy, not adversaries mapping an attack surface. The result is a model in production whose threats were never enumerated, protected by controls that were never chosen to stop anyone in particular.

Threat modelling an ML system is not a new discipline so much as an old one pointed at unfamiliar assets. The methods you already know still work - they just need a wider inventory of what can go wrong.

New assets, new attack surface

A classic threat model enumerates data stores, services, and trust boundaries. An ML system adds a set of assets that traditional models rarely list, and each one is a target in its own right.

  • Training data - poisonable, and often scraped from places you do not control.
  • Model weights - expensive to produce, stealable, and leaky of the data they were trained on.
  • The training and deployment pipeline - a software supply chain with all the usual compromise points.
  • The inference API - abusable for evasion, extraction, inversion, and denial-of-service.

Adapting the frameworks you already use

STRIDE still applies: spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, and elevation of privilege all have ML-specific expressions. Tampering becomes data poisoning; information disclosure becomes model inversion or membership inference; denial of service becomes resource-exhaustion of an expensive model. Attack trees still let you decompose a goal - "make the model misclassify this input" - into concrete steps. The framing is familiar; the leaves of the tree are what change.

Speak the shared language: MITRE ATLAS

Just as ATT&CK catalogues how adversaries behave against enterprise systems, MITRE ATLAS catalogues tactics and techniques against AI systems - reconnaissance of a model, crafting adversarial inputs, poisoning, extraction, and more. Using ATLAS gives your ML threat model a common vocabulary, a checklist of techniques to reason about, and a way to describe findings that other security professionals will immediately understand. It turns "the model might get attacked somehow" into a structured set of named, addressable techniques.

Building the model in practice

Start where you would for any system: draw the data flow, from raw training data through the pipeline to the deployed inference endpoint, and mark the trust boundaries - where does attacker-influenced data or input cross into your system? Then, for each ML asset, ask who the realistic adversaries are and what they gain: a competitor extracting your model, a fraudster evading your classifier, a privacy attacker recovering training data, a griefer running up your inference bill. Rank by impact and likelihood, and choose controls deliberately rather than reaching for whatever the platform enables by default.

Where governance meets engineering

A threat model is also the bridge between security engineering and AI governance. Regulators and internal risk functions increasingly want to know that you understood the risks of a model before deploying it - and a documented ML threat model, mapped to a framework like ATLAS, is exactly that evidence. Do it early, revisit it when the data or the model changes, and you replace hand-waving assurances with a defensible account of what you protected against and why. That, more than any single control, is what separates a governed AI system from a hopeful one.
