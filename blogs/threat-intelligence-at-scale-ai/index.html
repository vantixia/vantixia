Threat Intelligence at Scale: Letting AI Read the Firehose

No team can read every advisory, forum post and CVE. AI can - and can hand analysts the 1% that matters to them.

AI for Security By RAJNISH KUMAR • 21 Mar 2026 • 7 min read

Every threat intelligence programme eventually hits the same wall: the material worth reading grows faster than anyone's capacity to read it. Vendor reports, government advisories, ISAC bulletins, open-source feeds, researcher blogs - each one potentially relevant, most of them redundant, and all of them written for a general audience rather than your environment.

The collection problem was solved years ago. The comprehension problem was not, and that is precisely where large language models earn their keep.

Where the hours actually go

Watch a CTI analyst work and you'll find most of the day is not analysis. It's reading a forty-page report to confirm it says nothing new, copying indicators out of PDFs, deciding whether "targeting the energy sector in Europe" applies to a logistics firm in Manchester, and rewriting the same summary for three different audiences.

These are language tasks. They involve compressing, extracting, comparing and rephrasing text - which is what LLMs do better than any prior technology. Applied honestly, the goal is not machine-generated intelligence; it is giving analysts back the hours currently lost to mechanical reading.

Four jobs worth automating

The highest-return applications are narrow and unglamorous. Summarisation condenses long-form reporting into a structured brief: actor, victimology, tooling, and what changed since last time. Extraction pulls indicators and TTPs into structured formats your platform can ingest, turning prose into STIX objects or MITRE ATT&CK technique references. Clustering groups the fifteen write-ups of the same campaign so an analyst reads one synthesis instead of fifteen near-duplicates. Tagging maps each item against a profile of your estate.

That last one deserves emphasis, because relevance is where most intel programmes quietly fail. A model given a description of your technology stack, sectors, geographies and crown-jewel systems can score incoming reporting against it:

  • Does the tooling target software we actually run?
  • Does the victimology overlap with our sector or suppliers?
  • Do the TTPs land in gaps our detection coverage already struggles with?
  • Is this genuinely new, or a rehash of reporting we've already actioned?

Provenance is non-negotiable

The failure modes are predictable. Models mangle indicators - silently refanging a defanged domain, truncating a hash, or merging two IP addresses into one that appears nowhere in the source. They also blur attribution, presenting one vendor's medium-confidence assessment as settled fact.

The countermeasures are equally predictable. Validate every extracted indicator deterministically: a simple check that the string genuinely appears in the source document costs almost nothing and catches most fabrication. Keep a citation to the originating report attached to every claim that flows downstream. And preserve confidence language - "assessed with low confidence" must survive summarisation intact, or your intelligence platform slowly fills with false certainty.

Enriching the platform, not replacing the analyst

Pipe the validated output into your threat intelligence platform as enrichment: machine-generated summaries, estate-relevance scores and extracted objects attached to the original artefact, never replacing it. Anything destined to drive an automated control - a blocklist entry, a detection deployment, a firewall change - goes through human review first. The model shortens the queue; the analyst still signs off.

That review step also creates a feedback loop. When an analyst corrects a bad relevance score or a mangled extraction, log the correction. Those corrections become your evaluation set, and over time they tell you whether the pipeline has earned a wider remit or needs its leash shortened.

The bottom line

AI does not make threat intelligence smarter; it makes the firehose survivable. Treat the model as a tireless first reader - summarising, extracting, clustering and scoring relevance against your estate - with deterministic validation on every indicator and a human gate before anything touches production controls. Teams that adopt that shape stop skimming and start actually analysing, which was the point of the discipline all along.
