AI Red Teaming 101: A Field Guide to Breaking Language Models

Red teaming an LLM is not the same as pentesting a web app. Here is how to think like an adversary against a model that talks back.

Testing AI By RAJNISH KUMAR • 13 Jun 2026 • 8 min read

Traditional penetration testing rewards a certain kind of stubbornness: you find the one input the developer forgot to sanitise, and the bug reproduces every time. Language models refuse to play by those rules. The same prompt can pass on Monday and fail on Tuesday, and the "vulnerability" is often a sentence the model was talked into rather than a line of code someone left open.

That gap is why adversarial testing of AI systems has grown into its own discipline. Before you launch a first campaign, it helps to get clear on what the work actually involves and where it diverges from everything you already know.

Drawing the line around the work

Probing an AI system adversarially means stress-testing a model and everything wrapped around it – prompts, retrieval, tools, output handlers – to surface behaviour the operator would not sign off on. It is not the same as evaluation, which measures average-case quality on a fixed benchmark. This work hunts the worst case, and it assumes an intelligent opponent who adapts.

It is also not a one-time jailbreak contest. Coaxing a single edgy response proves little. The goal is a defensible account of which failure classes reproduce, how reliably, and under what conditions – evidence an engineering team can act on rather than a screenshot to post.

Three goals that pull in different directions

Useful campaigns keep three objectives distinct. Safety asks whether the system produces harmful content or advice. Security asks whether an attacker can make it violate its operator's intent – exfiltrate data, abuse a tool, escalate access. Robustness asks whether it degrades gracefully under malformed, adversarial, or simply strange input.

These goals conflict more often than newcomers expect. A model tuned to refuse aggressively scores well on safety and badly on usefulness; a permissive assistant does the reverse. Naming your primary objective up front stops you from grading the system against a standard nobody agreed to.

Personas and how you attack

Findings land harder when they trace to a realistic threat actor rather than a lab curiosity. Deciding whose shoes you are in shapes which attacks are even in scope.

  • The bored end user probing for policy limits with nothing but the chat box.
  • The malicious insider who already holds valid credentials and legitimate access.
  • The third party who controls content the model will later ingest – a web page, a document, an email.
  • The automated adversary running thousands of mutated prompts to find the one that slips through.

Manual testing and automated testing both belong here. Humans excel at the creative first break and at chaining context; automation excels at scaling a known technique and catching regressions cheaply. Strong programmes run both and let each cover the other's blind spot.

Scope, mindset, and the write-up

Scoping an AI engagement means agreeing on the target surface, the data the model can reach, whether tool calls fire against real systems, and what counts as a finding. Skip this and you will waste days arguing over whether a rude answer is a bug.

The mindset shift is the hard part. You are no longer proving a defect exists; you are estimating how often a probabilistic system misbehaves. That means reporting reproduction rates, not single transcripts – "this bypass succeeded in most attempts across these phrasings" says far more than one lucky screenshot. Good reports pair each finding with the exact prompts, the observed rate, the impact, and a concrete remediation the team can verify.

Where this leaves you

Red teaming an AI system borrows the adversarial instinct of classic offence but trades deterministic certainty for statistical honesty. Get the goals, personas, and scope straight before you start, treat every finding as a measured rate rather than a trophy, and write it up so an engineer can reproduce and fix it. Do that, and the rest of the discipline – methodology, tooling, specialised attacks – has somewhere solid to stand.
